# Lab L1.8 - Week 1 Group Assessment
## Instructor Answer Key (Instructor Only)

Use this as the facilitation guide, answer key, and grading reference for the Week 1 culminating assessment. The goal is not to force every team into one identical answer. The goal is to verify that students can reason from mission, enemy, terrain, evidence, and doctrine into a disciplined commander-ready plan.

Instructor note: This requires live infrastructure. Student outputs are planning artifacts, CAD products, and commander briefs.

## 0. Instructor Pre-Class Checklist

Before assessment start:

1. Confirm students have access to:
   - `L1.8 Student Brief.md`.
   - `supporting-documents/L1.8 Threat Actor Intelligence Sheet - Amber Tide.md`.
   - `supporting-documents/L1.8 Lunovia Network Map.png`.
   - Tactical planning worksheets.
   - ATT&CK, D3FEND, ENGAGE, and CAD reference material already used in Week 1.
2. Prepare one blank packet or shared workspace per team for:
   - Mission analysis.
   - METT-TC.
   - SALUTE.
   - DRAW-D.
   - Friendly and enemy OCOKA-W.
   - EMLCOA.
   - CG/CV.
   - ATT&CK-to-D3FEND/ENGAGE mapping.
   - CAD graph.
   - SOM, FSP, and task list.
3. Prepare one copy of `L1.8 Blank Student Rubric.md` per student or per LMS grading entry.
4. Assign teams of four when possible. Teams of three or five are acceptable.
5. Post the mission statement:

```text
Deny or degrade the adversary's ability to collect intelligence from the Lunovia Network in order to preserve friendly freedom of maneuver in cyberspace and generate viable options for pursuit.
```

6. Set visible time boxes for both work phases and both brief windows.
7. Remind students that the assessment is graded on evidence-based reasoning, usable planning products, and team execution, not on naming the most tools.
8. Do not repackage prior lectures, general scenario background, or previously issued framework handouts as L1.8 supporting documents. Students may use them as references, but the exercise-specific support packet should remain limited to the local threat intelligence sheet and network map.

## 1. Exercise and Access Model

This assessment does not use:

- a range VM;
- an ops-station-to-target foothold;
- a remote shell;
- a C2 server;
- live malware;
- Docker, local services, or cloud accounts.

Students operate from scenario materials, lecture notes, planning worksheets, and team-produced artifacts. If they use laptops, they are only for reading files, drafting packets, drawing graphs, and preparing briefs.

Do not require internet access. Do not require students to install software. If a team cannot access a reference site, provide the relevant ATT&CK, D3FEND, ENGAGE, or CAD handout already used during the week.

## 2. Initial Situational-Awareness Baseline

Because this is a tabletop assessment, the baseline is split into a scenario baseline and an optional classroom workstation baseline. The scenario baseline is part of the assessment. The workstation baseline is only for troubleshooting student access to documents.

### 2.1 Scenario Baseline

Have teams record these baseline facts before analysis begins:

```text
Friendly force: Lunovia cyber defense and supported government stakeholders.
Enemy force: Varenskian nation-state cyber actor described in the threat intelligence sheet.
Friendly terrain: Lunovia Network, including physical, logical, and persona-layer terrain shown in the network map.
Known problem: The network is compromised and adversary collection is ongoing or recently active.
Friendly mission: Deny or degrade adversary intelligence collection while preserving freedom of maneuver and pursuit options.
Current authority: Plan and recommend options; do not execute live actions during the assessment.
Required products: Phase 1 planning packet, Phase 2 execution products, CAD graph, commander briefs, and team contribution note.
```

Why this matters: students should begin by establishing who is friendly, who is enemy, what terrain matters, what authority exists, and what products are required. This replaces tool-first behavior with operational orientation.

### 2.2 Optional Classroom Workstation Baseline

If teams use a Linux or Windows workstation to write the packet, instructors may have them capture local context for troubleshooting file access only.

Linux/macOS:

```bash
date -Is
hostname
whoami
pwd
uname -a
id
w
ps -eo pid,ppid,comm,%cpu,%mem --sort=-%cpu | head
ss -tulpen 2>/dev/null | head -40
```

Windows PowerShell:

```powershell
Get-Date -Format o
hostname
whoami
Get-Location
$PSVersionTable
[System.Environment]::OSVersion.VersionString
query user
Get-Process | Sort-Object CPU -Descending | Select-Object -First 10 ProcessName, Id, CPU

Get-NetUDPEndpoint | Select-Object -First 30 LocalAddress, LocalPort, OwningProcess
```

Expected finding: local host output varies by classroom. Do not grade these commands. They only prove the student can identify time, host, user/session context, shell/runtime context, active users, processes, and sockets if workstation troubleshooting is needed.

## 6. Recommended Instructor Walkthrough

### 6.1 In-Brief and Team Formation (15 minutes)

Open with the mission statement and explain the two-phase model. Emphasize that students are being assessed on disciplined planning and commander-ready communication.

Expected student questions:

- What facts are fixed versus assumed?
- How detailed should the CAD graph be?
- Are teams allowed to identify multiple candidate options?
- How should RFIs be handled?

Instructor response: fixed facts come from the L1.8 student brief, local threat intelligence sheet, and local network map. Assumptions and RFIs are acceptable when clearly labeled. CAD should support the plan rather than become a standalone art project.

### 6.2 Phase 1 Work: METT-TC to CV (Approximately 3 hours)

Strong Phase 1 packets usually include:

- Mission analysis that restates task and purpose without losing the commander's intent.
- Enemy analysis that uses SALUTE for structured facts and DRAW-D for likely enemy choices on contact.
- Friendly OCOKA-W that identifies defender visibility, logs, authorities, support relationships, and service-continuity limits.
- Enemy OCOKA-W that explains how the adversary observes, hides, moves, collects, stages, and exits.
- Time and logistics analysis that addresses adversary tempo against friendly decision cycles.
- Civil considerations tied to government services, public trust, and the operational cost of disruption.
- EMLCOA that states enemy mission, current activity, and likely adaptation if pressured.
- CG/CV pair that is specific enough to shape the exploitation plan.

Acceptable EMLCOA range:

```text
The Varenskian actor is conducting intelligence collection against Lunovian government networks by using observed access, tooling, and infrastructure to locate and move mission-relevant information before defenders can convert detection into action. On contact, the actor is likely to shift infrastructure, reattempt access, accelerate collection, or reinforce access through alternate paths rather than abandon the mission immediately.
```

Acceptable CG statements include:

- The adversary's ability to maintain collection tempo across selected user personas, document stores, and outbound paths.
- The combination of access, staging, and egress that lets the actor move intelligence before defenders act.
- The actor's repeated access to mission-relevant terrain and the decision cycle that keeps collection ahead of response.

Acceptable CV statements include:

- Reliance on observable access, staging, or egress behavior that defenders can correlate faster than the adversary can adapt.
- Dependence on specific user personas or document repositories that can be monitored, constrained, or shaped.
- Dependence on infrastructure, timing, or behavior patterns that enable engagement or deception without immediately spooking the actor.

### 6.3 Phase 1 Briefs and Feedback (50 minutes)

Each team briefs for 8 minutes with 2 minutes of Q&A. Require teams to brief:

1. Enemy mission and current activity.
2. Key terrain.
3. EMLCOA.
4. CG/CV.
5. Top assumptions and RFIs.

Useful instructor questions:

- Which piece of evidence most strongly supports your EMLCOA?
- What would the enemy likely do if the first recommended action became visible?
- What makes your CV actionable rather than just descriptive?
- Which RFI would most change your Phase 2 plan?

During feedback, correct unsupported leaps before teams proceed to options development.

### 6.4 Phase 2 Work: Exploitation Plan to Tasks (Approximately 2.5-3 hours)

Strong Phase 2 packets usually include:

- ATT&CK mapping that begins from scenario behavior, not from technique memorization.
- D3FEND and ENGAGE options paired to each major behavior or behavior cluster.
- Clear option statements that include goal, expected effect on the CV, risk, authority, and evidence requirement.
- CAD graph with an adversary Agent node, two to three Attack nodes, at least one Artifact, at least one Condition, and one or more Countermeasures.
- Scheme of maneuver that phases observation, shaping, denial/degradation, pursuit enablement, and handoff.
- Fire support plan covering forensics, malware analysis, infrastructure, legal/authority, intelligence, communications, and partner coordination where relevant.
- Role-based tasks with "in order to" purpose statements, measures of performance/effectiveness, and supervision plan.

Acceptable option logic:

```text
If the CV is adversary dependence on observable staging and egress behavior, then an acceptable plan should prioritize evidence correlation, monitoring or shaping of staging paths, egress visibility, and options that expose, affect, or elicit adversary behavior before selecting a disruptive containment action.
```

Instructor teaching point: D3FEND and ENGAGE are planning vocabularies. They should not appear as isolated labels without explanation of how the option affects the CG/CV.

### 6.5 Phase 2 Briefs and Hot Wash (60 minutes)

Each team briefs for 8 minutes with 2 minutes of Q&A. Require teams to brief:

1. Exploitation plan and major ATT&CK mappings.
2. D3FEND/ENGAGE pairings and why they target the CV.
3. CAD graph and what it proves.
4. SOM phases.
5. FSP requirements and deconfliction.
6. Role-based tasks and measures.
7. Confidence and remaining risk.

Useful instructor questions:

- Which option best preserves friendly freedom of maneuver?
- Which option is most likely to spook the adversary, and how did you control that risk?
- What authority is required before the team acts?
- What evidence would prove the plan is working?
- Which task would you execute first if the commander approved the plan?

Close by collecting packets and contribution notes. If time permits, project one strong CAD graph and one strong task-purpose statement for the class debrief.

## 8. Acceptable Student Answers

There is no single required final plan. Award credit when teams make defensible, evidence-based arguments that respect the scenario and Week 1 doctrine.

Strong analytical answers:

- Separate evidence, assessment, assumptions, and RFIs.
- Explain why the adversary mission is intelligence collection rather than defaulting to generic disruption.
- Treat physical, logical, and persona terrain as operationally meaningful.
- Make CG/CV statements specific enough to guide action.

Strong framework answers:

- Map behavior to ATT&CK before selecting D3FEND or ENGAGE options.
- Explain the purpose and expected effect of each D3FEND/ENGAGE pairing.
- Use CAD to show relationships among agent, action, artifact, condition, and countermeasure.
- Preserve provenance instead of drawing unsupported graph edges.

Strong execution answers:

- Phase the plan so observation, shaping, denial/degradation, pursuit, and handoff are coherent.
- Identify support requirements and deconfliction points.
- State authority and risk before recommending actions that could reveal defender awareness.
- Assign role-based tasks with purpose and measures.

## 9. Common Mistakes

- Jumping straight to a tool or block action before completing EMLCOA and CG/CV.
- Treating ATT&CK as a checklist rather than a behavior mapping tool.
- Naming D3FEND or ENGAGE options without explaining the intended effect.
- Drawing a CAD graph that does not support the plan.
- Ignoring persona-layer terrain and stakeholder impact.
- Recommending disruptive action without authority, risk, or adversary reaction analysis.
- Producing a strong group packet while one or more students make no visible contribution.
- Briefing technical detail without commander-level decision logic.

## 10. Troubleshooting and Confirmation

### Teams Stall During Phase 1

Prompt them back to the operational baseline:

```text
What do you know?
What do you assess?
What assumption are you making?
What RFI would reduce the largest risk?
```

### Teams Cannot Choose a CG/CV

Ask whether the CV can shape Phase 2 actions:

```text
If this is the enemy's vulnerability, what can the friendly force observe, shape, deny, degrade, exploit, or hand off?
```

### Teams Jump to D3FEND or ENGAGE Too Early

Require the behavior trace:

```text
What observed behavior maps to ATT&CK, and why does that behavior make this D3FEND or ENGAGE option relevant?
```

### Teams Produce Weak CAD Graphs

Have them explain one edge at a time:

```text
What evidence proves this agent performed this action?
What artifact or condition makes the countermeasure relevant?
What would change if this edge were removed?
```

### Individual Contribution Is Unclear

Use Q&A and the contribution note to confirm each student's role:

```text
Which part of the packet did you own, what evidence did you rely on, and what changed after team feedback?
```

## 11. Link to Course Objectives

This assessment validates the Week 1 outcome: students can convert scenario evidence and doctrine into a disciplined tactical plan. It requires them to move from METT-TC through enemy analysis, terrain analysis, EMLCOA, CG/CV, exploitation planning, framework mapping, CAD, SOM, FSP, and tasks while producing a commander-ready handoff product.
